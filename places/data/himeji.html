Both a UNESCO World Heritage Site and a National Treasure of Japan, Himeji Castle (姫路城) in Hyogo prefecture is widely regarded as Japan's most spectacular and historically significant castle, often considered the finest example of Japanese feudal architecture even among the exclusive 12 Genson Tenshu (現存天守) , authentic Japanese castles with their main keep intact since their original construction. First established in the 14th century as a hilltop fortress, then extensively built upon in the early 17th century reaching its current castle form by 1609, Himeji Castle has survived wars, natural disasters, and modernization of the surrounding area, remarkably without losing its original form. Called Hakuro-jo or Shirasagi-jo, the "White Egret or Heron Castle," the castle is made of brilliantly white Shikkui , traditional Japanese plaster commonly used in castles, together with its layered wing-like roofs creating an appearance of lightness and grace, while its six-tier seven-story main keep, nearly 150-feet-tall, creates a sense of authority.

Often regarded as the two finest surviving castles in Japan, Himeji Castle and Matsumoto Castle in Nagano prefecture, known as Karasu-jo, or the "Crow Castle," are frequently compared for their historical authenticity and architectural excellence, but they differ strikingly not only in their colors but in their setting: with its dramatic backdrop of the Japanese Alps and a soaring height of nearly 100 feet, Matsumoto Castle majestically lies on flat lands, surrounded by a massive moat built for defense, while Himeji Castle, perched on top of Himeyama Hill, adding another 150 feet to its elevation, sits nearly 300 feet high from the base of the expansive castle complex, with an extensive network of outer walls and gates leading up to the main keep, designed to confuse invaders. Almost a visual opposite of each other but equally impressive in their own right, the two castles will feel almost poetic to those who appreciate old Japanese architecture.
