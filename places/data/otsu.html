An hour south from Tokyo, Kamakura (鎌倉) is the perfect destination for travelers looking to get a taste of Japan's pristine coastline, blended seamlessly with traditional and historic elements in a charming small-town atmosphere. This naturally scenic seaside town can be easily reached by affordable public transit from Tokyo and is steeped in culture and history, having served as the administrative capital of Japan's first Shogunate for about 150 years. Named after the city, Kamakura Period (1185–1333) marked the country's transition from the aristocratic rule from Kyoto during Heian Period (794-1185) to a feudal period dominated by Samurai , who tried to balance their military rule with tradition, culture, and religion, making this city a treasure trove of historic temples and shrines set among its hills.

Set between lush mountains and the beaches of Sagami Bay , Kamakura is also a top destination for those who love the outdoors, attracting hikers and surfers from all across Kanto and the world. On clear days or at sunset, Kamakura beaches – particularly Shichiri-ga-hama – offer majestic views of Mount Fuji, a scenary striking enough to be eternalized into an Ukiyo-e (woodblock prints) by the famed artist Hokusai in 1830 . In the hills, a network of trails lets you travel through the forests from one historic temple to another like a pilgrim in old Japan, surprising you at times with unexpected views of the ocean and Mount Fuji from a different angle. Most tourists visit as a day trip from Tokyo, but if you gravitate towards nature and relaxed small towns over the neon lights of Japanese cities, I recommend trying out Kamakura as a base like we did, which gives you the chance to enjoy all of its offerings, as well as nearby destinations in Kanagawa : Enoshima, famous for its seaside caves, shrines, and cliff-side ocean views; Yokohama, Japan's largest port city with waterfront attractions; Hakone, renowned for its tranquil lake scenery; and not to forget, easy day trips back to Tokyo by commuter trains in just an hour. As an Angeleno, I couldn't help but feel parallels between Kamakura and parts of the coastal areas of LA's Westside or South Bay: access to a relaxing coastline with abundant trails and a local culture, but still within reach of all the offerings of world-class cities nearby.

Vibes at a Glance

Favorite Things to Do

  • Stay on the Enoden train from Inamuragasaki to Koshigoe: If you're exploring Kamakura and its surroundings, you'll need to take Enoden's neighborhood trains, which have served the region since 1902. These old trains are slow and often crowded, and if you ride only from Kamakura Station to Hase – where several top sights are – you may even remember the ride as the worst part of the trip. But past Hase, from Inamuragasaki to Koshigoe, the trains exit out of the narrow neighborhood tracks and run along the coast, boasting panoramic ocean views for 10 minutes or so. If the time of day is right, it feels like pure magic.
  • Go hiking like a pilgrim: Kamakura is surrounded by lush mountains that are easily hikeable, but what makes them truly unique is that they are full of trails that connect the region's many hillside temples, shrines, and historic Yagura – caves and grottos – carved in during the 12th century. A few favorites: the Daibutsu trail, which will take you by the cave to Zeni-arai Benten and end at Jochi-ji; Mount Gion for grottos, Myohon-ji, and an overlook of Kamakura and the coast; Ten-en, "Sky Garden," for higher elevations; and Kamakura Hiromachi Forest for Fuji views on clear days.
  • Explore the hillside temples by Kita-Kamakura Station: When researching Kamakura, it's easy to focus on Kamakura Station and Hase, but these central areas, much like Kyoto, can end up being crowded like Disneyland. For a more calm and natural surrounding, take the JR local line to Kita-Kamakura Station, which feels like a completely different world. With a significantly more remote feeling, the area boasts some of the most serene temples in the region, like Jochi-ji, Meigetsu-in, Engaku-ji, and Kencho-ji.
  • Experience Central Kamakura: Of course, no trip to Kamakura would be complete without a visit to its core. Tsurugaoka Hachimangu is the most important shrine and is just a 10-minute walk from Kamakura Station. The two best streets to take to the shrine and back are Komachi-dori, a narrow street bustling with souvenir shops and restaurants, and Dankazura, an elevated path lined with Sakura trees and stone lanterns. At the shrine, don't miss the Bunkakan Museum, which has exhibitions and video that showcase all the historic festivals and ceremonies held at this shrine throughout the year, which really puts the shrine's significance into perspective.
  • Explore Hase's attractions: Also iconic to Kamakura, Hase is home to the Kamakura Daibutsu, "the Great Budda of Kamakura", and Hase-dera, a very polished temple that offers a variety of unique elements: a cave with carvings of the Benzaiten goddess, a massive wooden Kannon statue, koi and fall folliage, sweeping views of the Sagami Bay from the top of the complex, and Ajisai, hydrangea, that bloom the summer time. The neighborhood streets are also lined with local shops and eateries.
  • Take day trips from Kamakura: Kamakura is centrally located for exploring more of Kanagawa prefecture. Enoshima – part of Fujisawa, a mid-sized city with great urban amenities – is so close that it's often mistaken as Kamakura, while Yokohama and Odawara, another charming coastal city where you can transit to Hakone, can both be reached by a single train ride. For a more local Kanagawa vibe, take the Shonan Monorail to Ofuna or explore smaller towns like Zushi on the Miura Peninsula.
