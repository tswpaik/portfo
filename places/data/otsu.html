Just a 10-minute train ride from Kyoto by local commuter lines, Otsu (大津) – the capital of Shiga prefecture – is easily one of Japan's best-kept secrets. Flying under the radar thanks to its world-famous neighbor, Otsu sees fewer crowds of tourists compared to Kyoto, allowing visitors to experience its tranquil cultural heritage uninterrupted by over-tourism. As the heart of an ancient province called Omi , Otsu has a distinct identity as a political, cultural, and trading hub on the critical Tokaido route. For a brief five years in the country's turbulent 7th century, Otsu even held the title of Japan's capital with its own imperial palace, where the city's most important shrine, Omi Jingu , now sits. The defining feature of Otsu, however, is its access to Lake Biwa , Japan's largest lake. Shaped like the Japanese instrument Biwa , the lake starts with a narrower portion in the south around Otsu and expands like a balloon as it stretches north covering over 260 square miles, a surface area larger than the urban core of Tokyo and three times the size of Osaka. Estimated to be about 4-million-years-old, Lake Biwa is also one of the oldest lakes in the world, holding a unique and ancient biodiversity and, of course, historically serving as the primary source of fresh water, not only for Otsu and Shiga prefecture but all of the Kansai region. Today, the lake supplies virtually all of Kyoto's tap water through the historic Lake Biwa Canal built in 1890, a critical Meiji-era (1868-1912) industrialization effort said to have saved the declining economy of modern Kyoto after the capital was moved to Tokyo.

As the largest and most historic city on the lake, Otsu pays tribute to its grand nature, frequently referring to it as the "Mother Lake" . Surrounding Lake Biwa are the city's most culturally significant temples and shrines, with several offering sweeping views of the lake, like the historic Mii-dera (672) – home to 64 National Treasures – and Enryaku-ji (788) – a UNESCO World Heritage Site of Mount Hiei , one of two most sacred mountains in Japanese Buddhism alongside Wakayama's Mount Koya. The natural beauty of these lakeside temples and shrines were once famous enough to become a central subject of traditional Japanese art – immortalized as the Omi Hakkei or "Eight Views of Omi," an 18th century Ukiyo-e series painted by famed artists like Hiroshige . You can still visit most of these ancient places today and marvel at the scenery of Lake Biwa that inspired Japanese artists of the past. A few personal favorites:

  • Mangetsu-ji Ukimido (995) : a one-of-a-kind "Floating Hall" located in the lake itself around Otsu's Katata region
  • Ishiyama-dera (747) : a temple that sits on a stone mountain, where 11th-century author Lady Murasaki wrote the world-famous novel "Tale of Genji ," said to have been inspired by the moon's reflection on Lake Biwa
  • Karasaki Shrine (692) : known for its once majestic, but now declining lakeside pine, which provided a seed planted in 1837 at Kanazawa's Kenroku-en, one of the Three Great Gardens of Japan , giving rise to the garden's centerpiece named "Karasaki Pine"
