City of Remembrance & Resilience

Since the 16th century, Hiroshima (広島) has been an important population center for Japan and the Chugoku region. Known throughout the world, however, the city was devastated by an atomic bomb on August 6, 1945, along with Nagasaki shortly after , bringing an end to World War II. In the decades that followed, Hiroshima's resilient people rebuilt the city with remarkable determination and hope, establishing the city as the entire region's urban core once again and most importantly, a place to remember the victims, the consequences of war, and the importance of peace. Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park & Museum receives over two million visitors from around the world each year, including world figures like Barack Obama and Pope Francis, coming to pay respect and reflect on the importance of peace and nuclear disarmament.

Once again the largest city in the Chugoku region with around 1.2 million residents, Hiroshima today is a vibrant, modern city, bursting with unique character. Hiroshima Densetsu has the largest, most extensive and efficient network of streetcars in Japan, dating prior to the 1945 bombing. Some cars even survived the blast, and the company is known for resuming operations just three days after, serving as a symbol for recovery. Nicknamed "Moving Museums," many of the older streetcars you'll see in Hiroshima today come from all over Japan as cities like Kyoto, Osaka, and Fukuoka discontinued their tram lines and donated to the city. Throughout Hiroshima, you'll also find many places serving Okonomiyaki , quintessential Japanese soul food that both Hiroshima and Osaka claim as their local specialty, each adding their own flair to the dish.

Miyajima & Itsukushima Shrine

Located in the Seto Inland Sea just 13 miles southwest of the city of Hiroshima, Miyajima – "Shrine Island" or officially Itsukushima – is objectively one of the most iconic and striking sights, not only in Hiroshima prefecture or the Chugoku region but in all of Japan. One of Nihon Sankei , Japan's three most scenic views and often considered number one among the three, the island has long been a historic pilgrimage site and a famous sightseeing destination for domestic and international travelers alike.

At its heart stands Itsukushima Shrine , a UNESCO World Heritage Site famous for its iconic "floating" Torii gate that looks as if it floats on the water at high tide. While the Torii was built in 1875, the shrine itself, which also sits above shallow water, was originally founded in 593 and expanded in the 12th century. Despite its proximity to Hiroshima City, the site survived the atomic bomb virtually unscathed, and the shrine, which is constantly exposed to salt water and other natural elements, has been meticulously maintained for centuries – the majestic Torii, in particular, keeping up its vibrant and bold appearance for over 150 years now in the Seto Inland Sea. Like Nara, Miyajima is also famous for wild deer, considered sacred in Japanese culture, roaming freely around the shrine grounds and along the island's quaint shopping streets lined with small souvenir shops and local eateries.
